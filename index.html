Korea Population

Personal project · August 2026 · Interactive web tool

South Korea's fertility rate of 0.75 in 2024 is the lowest recorded anywhere. This simulator projects the country's population one year at a time, up to 100 years ahead, with a cohort-component model that ages each birth cohort forward using age-specific mortality, fertility, and migration. Readers can change the fertility rate, net migration, and time horizon and watch the population pyramid, dependency ratio, and median age respond together.

A second section combines the projection with age- and gender-specific wage and labor participation profiles to estimate the national taxable wage base. It separates the effect of a shrinking workforce from the effect of wage growth, with default assumptions taken from OECD data (0.7% annual real wage growth and the recent pace of gender pay gap closing). The page states which figures are sourced and which are modeled, and calls itself a teaching model rather than a fiscal forecast.

COVID-19 Severity

Columbia University · Data Science II (P8106) final project · Spring 2026 · R, caret, glmnet, gbm, ranger, kernlab, pROC

This project asked whether flexible machine-learning models beat simpler ones at predicting severe COVID-19 infection, and which participant characteristics drive that risk. I drew a 1,000-person sample, split it 80/20 with stratification on the outcome, and trained six classifiers with shared 10-fold cross-validation folds so the comparison was fair: logistic regression, elastic-net logistic regression, random forest, AdaBoost, and linear and radial SVMs.

All six models landed close together. The linear SVM and elastic net led on the held-out test set, so the added complexity of boosting and radial kernels did not buy meaningful accuracy. I used the elastic-net predicted probability as a continuous risk score. Variable importance and partial dependence plots from the random forest and AdaBoost models agreed that vaccination status was by far the strongest predictor, followed by systolic blood pressure, age, and BMI.

Breast Cancer PFS

Columbia University · Data Science II (P8106) midterm project · Spring 2026 · with Heather Lu and Clement Li · R, caret, glmnet, mgcv, earth

We built prediction models for log-transformed progression-free survival in breast cancer using demographic, tumor, and treatment characteristics from a 1990–1995 cohort, then tested how well the model held up on a 2000–2005 cohort with the same study design. We compared OLS, ridge, LASSO, elastic net, a generalized additive model, and MARS with 10-fold cross-validation. Their errors overlapped, so we chose LASSO for parsimony and interpretability.

Applied to the later cohort, the earlier model's error rose by about 21%. Refitting on the later cohort showed why: the HER2 coefficient moved from clearly negative to slightly positive, consistent with the arrival of HER2-targeted therapy, the hormonal therapy effect grew stronger, and the lymph node effect weakened.

World Cup Database

Columbia University · SQL final project · MySQL, CTEs, window functions, views

I designed a relational database of men's FIFA World Cup history from six raw CSV tables. Staging tables held the raw text; I then cleaned IDs, dates, and flags into a normalized schema of teams, players, matches, goals, appearances, and squads, with primary keys, foreign keys, and indexes, and loaded them in dependency order so every row kept referential integrity.

On top of the schema I wrote analytical queries for the 2014, 2018, and 2022 tournaments using CTEs, DENSE_RANK() and RANK() window functions, and a reusable view. A few findings: Mbappé and Messi tied for the most goals with 12 each; Croatia won all four of its penalty shootouts; the 90th minute produced 51 goals, more than four times any other minute; and France had the best record at 14 wins, 2 losses, and 3 draws.

Congestion Pricing

Columbia University · Pei Lab, Mailman School of Public Health · January 2026 – Present · Python (Google Colab), Advan/SafeGraph Weekly Patterns

NYC began charging a congestion pricing toll for driving into Manhattan below 60th Street on January 5, 2025. This project asks how that changed where people travel. I built a Python pipeline for this question, adapting an R workflow the group had used to study hurricane evacuation. It reads 68 weekly files of anonymized cellphone visit data, maps each visitor's home census tract to a ZIP code, corrects for visitors whose home is unknown, and spreads weekly counts across days.

The output is a daily NYC ZIP-code origin–destination dataset of 15.8 million rows, split into 7 business categories. I compared 30 days after the policy with a 13-day baseline before it and grouped the results by borough in heatmaps. The baseline overlaps the winter holidays, so these results are preliminary: some of the change may be seasonal rather than an effect of the policy.
